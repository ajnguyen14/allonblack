# Pitch (about 2½ minutes)

**[Problem, 20 s]** A casino dealer switches between a fair die and one loaded toward six. We never see which die is in play; we only hear the rolls. That's a textbook hidden Markov model: two hidden states, fair and loaded, with a sticky switching process and different emission probabilities. Our question is practical: *on the next roll, should we bet on six, and how much?*

**[Pipeline, 30 s]** The wearer has Meta glasses paired to an iPhone over Bluetooth. Meta's documented route for glasses audio is the hands-free profile, so the phone hears the announced number, recognizes "one" to "six" on-device, and turns it into an observation. If the glasses drop, it falls back to the phone mic, and you can always tap a number by hand. All the inference runs on the phone, and the answer comes back as a buzz in your pocket: nothing for hold, one short pulse for a small bet, two for a big bet. You never have to look at a screen.

**[The key idea, 45 s]** After every roll we run the forward algorithm, normalized at each step so it stays stable over thousands of rolls. It gives two different numbers, and we keep them apart on purpose. The *posterior*: how likely is it that the die that just rolled was loaded? And the *prediction*: push that belief through the transition matrix once, and how likely is the **next** die to be loaded? We bet only on the prediction, because the bet is on the next roll. In our demo sequence, 2, 6, 6, 1, 6, 6, after the fifth roll the current die is 70% likely loaded, but the next one is only 65% likely, because the dealer might switch back. So we get one buzz, not two. Viterbi is shown only in hindsight, because it uses future rolls.

**[Decision, 30 s]** With a 4-to-1 payout, the expected value of a dollar on six is 5·P(6) − 1. Break-even is P(6) = 20%. One interesting thing we found: under these parameters P(next loaded) can't fall below about 10.7%, so EV is *always* slightly positive. The 35% and 65% thresholds are a risk filter, not an EV filter. We also implemented a fractional-Kelly policy that sizes stakes by expected log-utility.

**[Evidence, 25 s]** We checked the filter against brute-force enumeration of every hidden path and an exact-fraction Python implementation, and they agree to 10⁻¹⁰. A simulated dealer drawing from the same HMM shows calibrated predictions: mean predicted P(6) 0.280 against 0.283 observed, and a better Brier score than assuming a fair die. That's our real-time HMM, from voice to vibration.
